Graphic Novel: Motherhood in Crisis by Laurence Ivil, Alicia Prager and Saidu Bah (Response)

The collection of readings in this graphic novel are, truly, unlike anything I have ever read before. There is such a raw vulnerability in each retelling, so much so, that you can’t help but ask yourself: What’s being done to change this crisis? Is there anything we can do to ensure better, more accessible resources for these women?

To be completely honest, when I first saw the comic-like illustrations, I was afraid that these images would take away too much attention from the personal stories of each woman… or worse, try to tone down their experiences. Thankfully, this was not the case. The simplicity of the graphic designs and the neutrality of the color palette never once overpowered the story. In fact, they helped elevate these women by allowing readers to visualize their struggles and individual lifestyles. I also greatly appreciated the video recordings and photography included in each reading, as this mixed media helped cement the actuality of these recountings. It was a reminder that each chapter of the graphic novel is a reality for many women out there and how, with enough trust and research, there are real treatments, caretakers, and facilities that are designed to help service them. The notes that close each chapter are also very significant. They inform people that the services meant to aid these unfortunate women are not as accessible or established as they should be. It’s a way to spread awareness, and maybe even encourage others to advocate for more facilities.

The story that most stuck out to me was Kadiatu’s, The Devil At My Door. Her willingness to share such an emotional, descriptive story, despite her own feelings of failure and grief, helped expose the significance of having proper healthcare available. I couldn’t come to terms with how the village had allowed itself to grow so reliant on the local healer, who never seemed to come up with an actual diagnosis or solution. I was appalled with how the deaths of Kadiatu’s previous children were simply disregarded as acts of the devil (and other benevolent forces), just because the healer could not come up with a logical explanation. Instead, he allowed Kadiatu to continue to lose her kids, with no understanding of how she could prevent the next death. When Kadiatu emphasized the length and cost of travelling to the hospital, I finally understood why so many of these pregnant women failed to take action against these unfathomable pains and burdens…it was because accessible treatment was an unknown concept to them. I mean, think about how much confusion and suffering they could avoid if there wasn’t such a stigma against hospitals. If there were more facilities out there with the necessary doctors and treatments.

This graphic novel has opened my eyes to a crisis I didn’t know much about, one that many Americans are privileged enough to ignore. Because the increase in dangerous outbreaks/crises across poorer regions is not a coincidence. It is due to lack: lack of awareness, lack of action, lack of care.
